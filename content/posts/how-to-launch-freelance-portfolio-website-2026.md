## TL;DR

- **One weekend is enough.** Saturday: domain, hosting, WordPress. Sunday: five pages and three case studies.
- **Five pages:** Home, Work, About, Services, Contact. Nothing else until those win you an inquiry.
- **Case studies beat thumbnails.** The problem, what you did, the result.
- **Know the four-year cost before you buy:** $143.52 upfront on Hostinger Premium, then $10.99/month at renewal. Both are before tax ([Hostinger](https://www.hostinger.com/pricing), read 12 October 2026). Checkout adds local tax: the same plan showed $169.35 at checkout in India.
- **Give the site a keep-or-cut rule**, the same as every other subscription in your tool stack.

If you're a freelance copywriter, designer, VA or coach, you've probably done the audit at least once: the card statement, read line by line, every subscription cost questioned. Is this one earning its keep, or do I cancel it? A portfolio website is the one line you can't judge until it exists, so most solo freelancers put it off for months.

You don't need months. You need one weekend, five pages, and a subscription cost you've worked out before you sign up, not after the renewal email arrives. On Hostinger's Premium plan, four years of hosting is **$143.52 paid upfront, before tax**, which works out to $2.99 a month, and it renews at **$10.99 a month** after that ([Hostinger](https://www.hostinger.com/pricing), read 12 October 2026). The renewal number is the one most guides leave out, and it decides whether the site feels like an asset or one more charge you resent. Tax is the other number: the checkout total can be higher than $143.52, as the cost table below shows.

## Who this guide is for

You run a one-person service business. You already pay for Google Workspace, Canva, a scheduling tool and probably a few things you've forgotten about. You get most of your work from referrals, a marketplace profile, or both, and you've noticed two things: referrals search your name before they reply, and marketplace profiles can be suspended, reranked or buried by a rule change you had no say in.

If that's you, the case for a portfolio site isn't "look professional." It's that a site on your own domain is the one place a referred client can check you out that no platform can switch off.

## Saturday morning: decide what the site must do

A freelance portfolio has three jobs, and every decision this weekend serves one of them:

1. **Prove you're real and competent:** your name, your face or brand, a clean look.
2. **Show work aimed at the clients you want next:** not every project you've done.
3. **Make it easy to hire you:** an obvious next step on every page.

If something you're tempted to add doesn't serve one of those three, it waits.

## Saturday: register a domain and set up hosting

**Domain.** Use your name, or your studio name: `yourname.com` if it's free, otherwise a clean variant. Skip hyphens, numbers and extensions you'd have to spell out on a call. Most annual hosting plans include the domain free for the first year, so you can register both in one checkout. Note that the domain renews separately after year one.

**Budget for tax on Saturday, not after it.** The $143.52 for Hostinger's four-year Premium plan is a pre-tax price ([Hostinger](https://www.hostinger.com/pricing), read 12 October 2026). Set aside a bit more, because checkout adds local tax. In India, the same plan showed $169.35 on the checkout page (see the cost table above). Check your own total before you click pay.

**Hosting and platform.** WordPress on your own hosting gives you the lowest long-term cost and content you can move anywhere. The setup used to be the scary part. ## Saturday afternoon: build the five pages

- **Home:** who you are, who you help, and one call to action above the fold. "Freelance email copywriter for SaaS teams. Book a 20-minute call." is enough.
- **Work:** your case studies (Sunday's job).
- **About:** the person behind the work, a real photo, and why a client should pick a solo operator over an agency.
- **Services:** what you offer in the client's words, not yours, with a clear way to start.
- **Contact:** the shortest possible route to reaching you.

More pages don't make a solo freelancer look bigger. They make the site slower to build and slower to load.

## Sunday: write three case studies

This is the page that wins or loses the client, and the usual mistake is treating it like a gallery. Thumbnails show you can make things. Case studies show you solve the problem the client is paying to make go away.

For each project, write three short parts:

1. **The problem:** for example, "the client's onboarding emails weren't getting clicks and nobody on their team owned them."
2. **What you did:** your specific role, not the whole team's.
3. **The result, with a number if the client will let you share one:** a launch hit on time, a shorter turnaround, more signups. Ask before you publish a client's figures.

Three relevant case studies beat twenty random samples. Pick the work that matches the clients you want next, not the work you're proudest of.

## Sunday afternoon: make it obvious how to hire you

- Put a call to action on every page: book a call, request a quote, or email you.
- Keep the contact form to name, email and message. Every extra field is another reason to close the tab.
- Send yourself a test message before launch. A contact form that silently fails loses every lead and you'll never know.
- You probably already pay for a scheduling tool. Put that link on the Contact page so a prospect can book without three emails of back and forth.

## The SEO and speed basics, in 30 minutes

- Install an SEO plugin to manage titles, meta descriptions and your sitemap. On WordPress, the free version of [Rank Math](/go/rankmath/) covers this.
- Keep it fast. Google's Core Web Vitals treat a page as good when its main content loads within 2.5 seconds, it responds to a tap within 200 milliseconds, and its layout doesn't jump around ([web.dev, Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). A lightweight theme and a caching plugin get most portfolio sites there.
- Write your Services page for the work you actually want, for example "freelance email copywriter for SaaS", so you can be found for that and not for everything.

## Pre-launch checklist

- Your name and what you do are clear on the first screen of the homepage.
- The Work page has at least three case studies with a result.
- Every page has an obvious next step.
- The contact form reaches your inbox (you tested it).
- The site loads over HTTPS and is fast on your phone.
- Search engines can see it (WordPress has a "discourage search engines" box; make sure it's unticked).

## Give it a keep-or-cut rule

Every other subscription in your stack should earn its place, and this one should too. You keep paying for hosting whether or not anyone visits, so decide now what "earning its keep" means for your business, write it down, and check it on a date in your calendar.

A workable rule: link the site from your email signature, your marketplace profiles and every proposal for six months. If it hasn't produced a single inquiry or helped close a single referral by then, the hosting isn't the problem. The Work page is. Rewrite the case studies for the clients you want before you cancel anything.
